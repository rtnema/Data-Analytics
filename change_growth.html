How Do We Read a City's Growth?

Six ways of asking the same question — how much did Bengaluru change? — each one built from the last. Work through the tabs in order, then try the same maths on Hosur and Mumbai in the worksheet.

Absolute difference between consecutive census years

Bengaluru's population was counted every ten years. The simplest question we can ask: how many more people were there each time?

The logic
Subtract the earlier year's population from the later year's population. Because the years are already in order, we never need to worry about the sign — the answer is always how many people were added in that decade.
Difference = Population(later year) − Population(earlier year)

Absolute difference between any two years

The same subtraction works for non-consecutive years too — useful when you want to compare, say, the India of 1971 with the India of 2011 directly, skipping the decades in between.

The logic
Pick any two years. Subtract the smaller population from the larger one — or just take the absolute value of the subtraction, so the order you pick the years in doesn't matter.
Difference = | Population(Year B) − Population(Year A) |

    Percentage change from the previous census

    A raw difference doesn't tell you if the growth was fast or slow relative to how big the city already was. Percentage change fixes that by comparing the difference to the earlier population.

    The logic
    Take the same decade-on-decade difference from Tab 1, but instead of leaving it as a raw number, divide it by the earlier year's population and multiply by 100. This turns the change into "out of every 100 people who lived here, how many more arrived."
    % change = [(Population(later) − Population(earlier)) / Population(earlier)] × 100

    Percentage change between any two years

    Same formula as Tab 3, generalised — you're no longer restricted to comparing a year with the one right before it.

    The logic
    Choose a start year and an end year. The percentage change tells you how much the population grew (or, in principle, shrank) over that whole stretch, relative to where it started.
    % change = [(Population(end) − Population(start)) / Population(start)] × 100
    RESULT

      CAGR — Compound Annual Growth Rate

      Percentage change over 50 years tells you the total growth, but not the yearly pace. CAGR answers: "if this city had grown by the exact same percentage every single year, what would that yearly percentage have to be?"

      The logic
      This is the same idea as compound interest on a bank deposit. We find the ratio of the ending population to the starting population, then take the n-th root of it (where n is the number of years between them) to spread that total growth evenly across every year, then subtract 1 to turn it into a growth rate.
      CAGR = [ (Population(end) / Population(start)) ^ (1/n) − 1 ] × 100, where n = end year − start year
      STEP BY STEP

        Compare this to the plain % change in Tab 4 for the same years — CAGR is always the smaller, "smoothed-out" number, because it's a yearly rate rather than a 50-year total.
